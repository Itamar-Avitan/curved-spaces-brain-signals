What a BCI actually does

Part 1 of 5

Curved things break flat arithmetic

Not “space can bend” — that is the famous version and it is not the useful one. The useful version is what curvature does to ordinary sums and averages, because in Part 2 the thing being averaged is a brain recording.

1.1

Gauss found this problem while surveying land.

Euclid’s geometry assumes the space is flat, and for two thousand years that was the only geometry there was.

Gauss, running the Hanover land survey in the 1820s, proved something sharper than “surfaces can curve”: curvature is measurable from inside the surface, without ever stepping outside it — and therefore no flat map of a curved surface can keep all its distances right. Every world map you have ever seen is lying about something, and it has to.

Riemann generalised it to spaces of any dimension — and, crucially, to spaces whose “points” are not places at all: geometry only needs a way to say what “near,” “far,” and “between” mean, and once you have that, it applies just as well to a collection of data tables as it does to a landscape.

Einstein used it, decades later, to describe gravity, and that application is what made the whole idea famous. But fame bought the picture, not the arithmetic — most people meet a bending sheet and never meet what bending does to a sum. That is the wrong order, and it is what the rest of this part corrects.

1.2

A flat map is exact where you centre it, and wrong by more the further you go.

That is the whole transferable idea, and it is worth more than the picture of a bending sheet. Flattening does not fail everywhere at once. It fails gradually, from a single point outward — and you get to choose the point.

Drag the second point away from the centre below. At the centre the two rulers return the same number. They are not approximately equal there; they are equal. Then watch what happens as the gap grows.

Remember this one. It comes back twice: it is why one of the two BCI decoders in Part 4 works at all, and why the fix for tomorrow’s recording session in §4.4 is a one-line change.

1.3

Einstein’s gravity is the most famous case of choosing a distance rule.

§1.2 showed two rulers giving different answers once you step away from the centre — measuring distance is a choice. Matter makes that choice for spacetime, curving the geometry near mass, and objects then follow the straightest path available through it — a geodesic. The rule that says how to measure a single step at each location is the metric.

g
The metric: a distance rule

It tells us how to measure a tiny step at each location.

The path looks curved to us, but it is locally as straight as the geometry allows.

1.4

Where the analogy stops: our surface bends the other way, and that is lucky.

A globe has positive curvature: two nearby straightest paths — geodesics — curve back together as you follow them. A gravity well bends paths the same way, which is what gravity pulling things together looks like in this picture. The space of covariance matrices, with the ruler that Part 3 chooses, bends the other way instead: nearby paths pull apart rather than reconverge, and that is what non-positive curvature means here.

That sign is not trivia. It buys:

  • Exactly one shortest path between any two points.
  • No cut locus.
  • A class centre that always exists and is always unique.
  • An algorithm that finds that centre and always converges.

On a sphere, none of this holds.

The causal order runs one way, and it is worth saying plainly: we need invariance → that forces this metric → that metric makes the space curved → the curvature is non-positive → therefore means and geodesics are unique → therefore the algorithms are simple. Curvature is a cost that turns out to be a benefit. It is not what makes the decoder accurate.

Part 2 of 5

An EEG trial is a shape, not a squiggle

Point that geometry at the brain. A few seconds of signal across many electrodes folds into one structured object — and that object, not the waveform, is what a modern decoder actually works with. Between 2014 and 2016 pipelines built on it took first place in five international neural-decoding competitions, against fields of 260 to 688 teams.

2.1

One trial becomes a table of which electrodes moved together.

A single trial is thousands of voltage samples across dozens of electrodes — far too much to compare, trial to trial, by eye. Instead of keeping every sample, summarise how every pair of electrodes changes together over the trial. That summary is a covariance matrix: not a recording of the signal, but a table built from it.

The diagonal of that table is how much each channel varies on its own. The off-diagonal is how much two channels vary together — and that is where the motor signal actually lives. Imagining a left-hand squeeze changes how motor-cortex electrodes move together, not merely how loudly any one of them fires alone.

Turn up the channel relationship in the widget below and watch the ellipse tilt. That tilt is not standing in for the off-diagonal number — it is the off-diagonal number, drawn.

Every one of these tables is symmetric, and every one reports a real amount of variation in whichever direction you look — never zero, never negative. Tables with both properties have a name: symmetric positive-definite.

2.2

Not every table is possible, so they fill a curved cone.

Positivity is a real constraint, not paperwork. Push one off-diagonal entry far enough relative to the diagonal and one of the matrix’s eigenvalues crosses zero and turns negative — and at that instant the table stops describing any variation a real recording could produce, in any direction. It has stopped being a covariance matrix at all.

The tables that survive this constraint do not fill all of space. They fill one specific region of it — the SPD manifold — shaped like a cone, with every valid covariance matrix sitting at exactly one point inside it. Choose a rule for measuring distance across that region, and the region itself starts behaving like a curved space rather than a flat one.

That cone, not a sphere or a metaphor, is the actual object the rest of this guide works inside: every EEG trial is one point in it, and Part 3 picks the rule for how far apart two of those points are allowed to be.

Part 3 of 4

Using it: the decoder

With every trial now a point, a decoder needs three moves: measure distance, build a class center, predict the nearest one. Then two questions that decide whether it survives contact with real hardware — why this distance, and what happens tomorrow.

Move 1 · measure distance

What should “halfway” between two EEG patterns look like?

Before a decoder can average trials, compare them, or align two sessions, it has to choose a rule for distance between covariance matrices. The obvious rule — treat each matrix as a flat list of numbers and measure the straight-line gap — quietly distorts the answer. Here is exactly how it goes wrong.

The case against the straight ruler Average two equal-strength patterns the straight (Euclidean) way and the result balloons — it invents scale that was in neither.

Watch the relative area below: the entry-wise midpoint grows past 1.00 while the geometry-aware one holds it. And a distorted average is a distorted class center — which decides every prediction. This one choice sits under the whole decoder.

Move 2 · build a class center

How can many EEG trials become one representative pattern?

Repeated trials from the same BCI class are similar but never identical. We need one center that summarizes them using the same distance rule that will later judge a new trial.

Plain idea: the center by curved-space distance Mathematical name: Riemannian mean
The sentence to leave with A class center is whatever sits at the smallest total distance from its examples.

Each ellipse is already a covariance summary of one trial. The chosen center becomes the stored prototype for that class.

What “the center” actually means

Why the examples use ellipses

Two-channel diagonal covariance matrices can be drawn as simple ellipses, so every calculation remains visible. Real EEG uses larger, full covariance matrices; the same geometric definitions extend to those higher-dimensional objects.

Move 3 · make the prediction

From an EEG trial to a BCI decision.

We now have a structured summary for every trial, a meaningful class average, and a distance rule. That is enough to build a complete classifier.

Plain idea: choose the nearest learned class center Mathematical name: Minimum Distance to Mean (MDM)
The sentence to leave with To label a new trial, measure it against each class center and take the nearest one.

This is the decision the BCI actually ships: measure a new trial’s distance to every class prototype and return the nearest label.

The keystone · why this ruler

The measurement lies. The geometry is built to ignore the lie.

Swelling is one reason to distrust the straight ruler. Here is the other, and it is the one that decides whether a decoder survives contact with real hardware.

Plain idea: rewire the recording and the distance shouldn’t care Mathematical name: affine invariance
The sentence to leave with Rewire the amplifier and the straight-line distance changes. The Riemannian one does not move at all.

Volume conduction, electrode gain, and your choice of reference all do the same thing to a covariance matrix. Try each one below and watch which ruler notices.

The ruler that would not move

One family, one fix

Volume conduction, the lead field, electrode gain, re-referencing, whitening, spatial filtering, session drift — every one of them multiplies your covariance matrix on both sides by the same kind of invertible matrix — a congruence. Because the affine-invariant distance is blind to that whole family, you get much of the benefit of untangling the signal without ever having to untangle it.

The other route · geometry meets machine learning

A curved surface looks flat when you stay nearby.

MDM decides by comparing a trial to class centers. The other common route flattens the curved space first: near one reference point it behaves like ordinary flat space, and standard linear classifiers can read the patterns there.

Plain idea: flatten the neighborhood around a reference Mathematical name: tangent space, reached by the log map
The sentence to leave with Flatten around one reference point and every trial becomes a plain list of numbers that ordinary machine learning can read.

The log map turns each nearby covariance matrix into one vector, so tools like LDA, logistic regression, or an SVM can take over from here. The same re-centering on a reference also makes two recordings line up — which is how a decoder transfers to a new session or person with little recalibration, the geometry’s quiet superpower.

The payoff · why any of this was worth it

A decoder that survives tomorrow.

Everything so far has been about one recording. The reason the field cares about this geometry is what happens across two — because in practice a BCI is unusable if every session starts with twenty minutes of recalibration.

Plain idea: slide each session onto the same starting point Mathematical name: re-centering (Riemannian alignment)
The sentence to leave with Re-centering each session on its own average cancels the difference between sessions without damaging what is inside them.

Drag the drift up to break the decoder, then re-center and watch it come back — without a single label from the new session.

One line, and it needs no labels

And when it doesn’t help

The demo above is synthetic, and its numbers were chosen to show the mechanism. The companion notebook runs the same move on real recordings and gets a negative result: the run centers collapse together exactly as they should, and the accuracy does not improve. The three runs there came from one sitting, so there was no session shift to remove — and re-centering threw away the small real differences along with the nuisance that wasn’t there. Alignment is a hypothesis about your data, not a free upgrade. It pays when what separates your recordings really is a change of hardware. Measure it; don’t assume it.

In practice · choose the tool for the job

Geometry is a family of workflows, not one classifier.

These methods share covariance representations but use them differently. A credible BCI study compares them with a strong task-specific baseline.

The sentence to leave with These methods share a representation and differ in what they learn from it — so the right one depends on how much calibration data you can ask for.

MDM, tangent-space models, and CSP + LDA trade interpretability, flexibility, and calibration cost differently — match the method to the data you have.

Method What it learns Useful when Watch for
Riemannian MDM One covariance center per class Calibration is limited and interpretability matters A simple center may miss complex class boundaries
Tangent-space model A linear boundary in geometry-aware features Classes need a more flexible separator Reference points and preprocessing must stay inside each training fold
CSP + LDA Spatial filters with class-separating variance Motor imagery has two classes and enough calibration data CSP can overfit with few trials and must be fitted inside validation

What geometry does not solve

A better ruler cannot repair a bad measurement.

01

Artifacts

Eye movements, muscle activity, and poor electrode contact can dominate covariance.

02

Changing distributions

EEG shifts across time, sessions, devices, and participants; re-centering may help, but it has to be tested rather than assumed.

03

Weak validation

Randomly mixing nearby trials can leak recording-specific information and inflate performance. Hold out whole runs instead.

04

Missing neurophysiology

A mathematically valid feature is not automatically relevant to the task or user.

…but geometry can also guard the signal

The Riemannian “potato”: an automatic quality check.

The same covariance matrices double as a live signal-quality score. A trial whose covariance sits too far — in Riemannian distance — from a clean reference is flagged and skipped, so the decoder never acts on a blink or a muscle burst. One representation, two jobs: make the call, and know when not to.

What this example shows

Geometry can make limited data more useful.

With only two training trials per class, the covariance-based methods were more accurate than CSP here. Give every method plenty of calibration data and the ordering flips — CSP + LDA edges ahead at ten trials per class. The honest summary is not “Riemannian wins”; it is that it needs less from the user to get going.

Riemannian MDM 94.5%
Tangent space 94.9%
